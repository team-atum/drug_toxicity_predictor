Scientific Foundation

Built on the fundamentals of toxicology

We did not treat this as a pure data science problem. We modelled toxicity the way a pharmacologist would — from first principles.

Step 1 — Understanding what toxicity actually is

Toxicity is not a binary property of a molecule — it is the result of a molecule interacting with a specific biological target in a specific tissue at a sufficient concentration. The same compound can be therapeutic at one dose and lethal at another (Paracelsus: "the dose makes the poison"). We accounted for this by mapping each of the 12 Tox21 assays to specific biological pathways and organ systems, so our output is not just "toxic/safe" but which pathway is disrupted and which organ is at risk.

Dose-response relationship Receptor binding Pathway disruption Organ specificity

Step 2 — Choosing the right molecular representation

A molecule's toxicity is determined by its 3D shape, electron density, and functional groups — not just its molecular formula. We encode each compound using Morgan circular fingerprints (ECFP4, 2048 bits) which capture local chemical environments up to 2 bond radii — the same representation used in medicinal chemistry for decades. We augment this with 7 physicochemical descriptors — MolWt, LogP, TPSA, H-bond donors/acceptors, rotatable bonds, and aromatic rings — which directly encode drug-likeness and membrane permeability.

ECFP4 Fingerprints Lipinski Rule of Five LogP — lipophilicity TPSA — membrane crossing RDKit Descriptors

Step 3 — The Tox21 assay framework and what it tests

The NIH Tox21 program tested ~10,000 compounds across 12 high-throughput screening assays designed to detect specific toxicity mechanisms. We train a dedicated XGBoost model per assay, then synthesize results into organ-level risk assessments. This is fundamentally different from a single binary "toxic/safe" classifier — we predict how and where toxicity manifests.

Nuclear receptor pathways Stress response pathways Endocrine disruption Genotoxicity Mitochondrial toxicity

Step 4 — Handling class imbalance as a scientific reality

In the real world, toxic compounds are the minority — most chemicals tested are inactive. A naive model learns to say "safe" for everything and still achieves 90% accuracy. We handle this using scale_pos_weight in XGBoost — the exact ratio of negative to positive samples — ensuring the model is calibrated to the true prior probability of toxicity, not the shortcut of predicting the majority class.

scale_pos_weight balancing ROC-AUC evaluation Stratified train/test split

Step 5 — Interpretability as a scientific requirement, not a feature

A black-box toxicity prediction is scientifically useless to a medicinal chemist. They need to know which part of the molecule is causing the flag so they can modify it. We integrate SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) — a game-theoretic method that guarantees fair, complete attribution of every prediction to individual molecular features. For the first time, a chemist can see exactly which structural pattern is driving the toxicity call and make targeted modifications.

What Makes ATUM Different

Six things no other student tool does

01

Multi-assay, not multi-label

We train a dedicated XGBoost model for each of the 12 Tox21 assays independently, respecting the biological specificity of each pathway rather than collapsing everything into a single multi-label classifier that loses mechanistic signal.

02

Assay → Organ → Risk cascade

Every assay result feeds into a weighted organ risk scoring system. An assay marked "high severity" contributes more to its mapped organ's risk score than a "medium severity" one — the output mirrors how a clinical toxicologist would interpret the same data.

03

SHAP for structural insight

Judges will ask "why does your model say this?". We are the only team that can answer this question per-compound, per-feature, with mathematical guarantees of fairness via Shapley game theory. No other team will have this.

04

Real 3D geometry from SMILES

Our 3D viewer doesn't display a static diagram — it generates actual energy-minimised 3D coordinates using MMFF force-field optimisation in RDKit, then renders them in Jmol standard coloring. Every novel compound gets a unique, scientifically valid 3D structure.

05

Dosage and excretion context

We augment predictions with rule-based pharmacokinetic context derived from molecular properties: predicted physical state warnings (solid/liquid/gas), dosage range context based on Lipinski parameters, metabolic risk flags, and excretion pathway prediction based on LogP.

06

Works on undiscovered molecules

Our model generalises to any valid SMILES string — including hypothetical compounds that have never been synthesised. Morgan fingerprints encode structural patterns, not compound identities, so the model can extrapolate to novel chemical space with meaningful confidence.

Research Basis

Grounded in published science

Every design decision in ATUM traces back to peer-reviewed research in computational toxicology and cheminformatics.

Dataset Foundation

NIH Tox21 Challenge Dataset

The Tox21 program was a collaboration between NIH, EPA, and FDA to develop better toxicity assessment methods using high-throughput screening. The dataset covers ~10,000 compounds across 12 assays and is the gold standard benchmark for in-silico toxicology.

Huang R. et al., "Tox21Challenge to Build Predictive Models of Nuclear Receptor and Stress Response Pathways," Front. Environ. Sci., 2016
Molecular Representation

Morgan Circular Fingerprints (ECFP4)

Developed by Morgan (1965) and formalised by Rogers & Hahn (2010), ECFP4 fingerprints encode the local chemical environment of each atom up to radius 2. They are the most widely used molecular representation in drug discovery and have been validated across thousands of QSAR studies.

Rogers D. & Hahn M., "Extended-Connectivity Fingerprints," J. Chem. Inf. Model., 50(5), 742–754, 2010
ML Model Choice

XGBoost for Imbalanced Bioactivity Data

Gradient-boosted decision trees have consistently outperformed deep learning on tabular molecular descriptor data with class imbalance — the exact setting of Tox21. XGBoost's scale_pos_weight parameter provides principled handling of the sparse toxicity signal in screening datasets.

Chen T. & Guestrin C., "XGBoost: A Scalable Tree Boosting System," KDD 2016
Interpretability Method

SHAP — Shapley Additive Explanations

SHAP is grounded in cooperative game theory. It is the only attribution method that simultaneously satisfies local accuracy, missingness, and consistency axioms — guaranteeing that feature attributions are mathematically fair. This is critical for a safety-relevant application like toxicity prediction.

Lundberg S. & Lee S-I., "A Unified Approach to Interpreting Model Predictions," NeurIPS, 2017
Drug-Likeness Descriptors

Lipinski's Rule of Five

Lipinski's rules (MolWt < 500, LogP < 5, H-donors < 5, H-acceptors < 10) define oral bioavailability boundaries. We use these same descriptors as features because they encode fundamental pharmacokinetic properties that strongly correlate with both absorption and toxicity risk.

Lipinski C.A. et al., "Experimental and computational approaches to estimate solubility and permeability," Adv. Drug Deliv. Rev., 1997
3D Structure Generation

MMFF Force-Field Optimisation

The Merck Molecular Force Field (MMFF94) is the standard for small-molecule geometry optimisation in computational chemistry. We use RDKit's implementation of ETKDGv3 for initial embedding followed by MMFF minimisation — the same pipeline used in industrial virtual screening.

Halgren T.A., "Merck Molecular Force Field," J. Comput. Chem., 17(5–6), 490–519, 1996
